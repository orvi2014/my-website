Every argument about the greatest football team ever collapses into the same problem within about four minutes. Someone says Brazil 1970. Someone says the eras cannot be compared. Someone mentions that today's average full-back is fitter than 1970's best forward, and the conversation dies without resolving anything.

The comparison problem is real, but it has a solution most lists refuse to use. Stop comparing teams across eras. Compare each team to the field it actually played, then rank the size of the gap. A side that beat its contemporaries by a wider margin than any other side beat theirs has the strongest claim, whatever decade the boots came from.

Ranked that way the order shifts, some famous teams fall, and a team that won nothing at all climbs into the top three.

## What actually makes a football team the greatest?

Four things, and I weight them in this order.

Margin over the field comes first. Goal difference, win rate, and the calibre of the opponents beaten. A dominant record against weak opposition is worth less than a narrow one against strong opposition, so the raw numbers have to be adjusted before they mean anything.

Then tactical originality. Did the team solve something nobody else had solved yet? Copying an idea well is a lesser achievement than inventing it, because the inventor was beating opponents who had no prepared answer.

Durability matters nearly as much. One tournament is a small sample, and football is noisy enough that a good side gets hot for six matches maybe once a decade. Sustained dominance across years is much harder to fake.

Influence comes last, and it is the softest of the four. It asks whether the sport reorganised itself around what the team did. I keep it in because it separates teams that won from teams that changed what winning looks like, but I do not let it outvote the evidence.

What is missing from that list is trophies as a standalone number. Trophies measure outcomes at the end of knockout tournaments, and knockout football is close to a coin flip between evenly matched sides. A team can be the best in the world for four years and win nothing, which is exactly what happened to two of the sides below.

## Brazil 1970: the widest margin anyone has produced at a World Cup

Six matches, six wins, 19 goals scored, seven conceded. Jairzinho scored in every match, which had never been done before and has not been done since. Their knockout opponents were England, Uruguay and Italy, three of the four most decorated nations in the sport at that point, and they beat Italy 4-1 in the final.

The tactical case is stronger than the nostalgia case. Mário Zagallo was running fluid positional rotation a year before Rinus Michels codified Total Football at Ajax, and doing it without the vocabulary that would later be invented to describe it. The team shifted between 4-2-4, 4-3-3 and 3-4-3 by phase of possession without a coach signalling any of it.

The honest counterargument is that the 1970 tournament had 16 teams and Brazil played six matches, none of which went to extra time. Their physical reserves were never tested the way a modern side's are. That weakens the record without breaking it. [The full case for and against Brazil 1970, including where modern pressing benchmarks expose them](/chapters/football/was-the-1970-brazil-side-actually-the-greatest-team-ever-assembled-the-data-says-yes-but), is worth reading before you accept or dismiss the verdict, because the weakness in that side is specific and it is not the one people usually name.

## Hungary 1950-1954: the highest-ranked team that won nothing

The Mighty Magyars did not lose a match between June 1950 and July 1954. The record across that span reads 42 wins, seven draws and one defeat, and the defeat was the World Cup final itself. They beat England 6-3 at Wembley in 1953, the first time England had ever lost at home to a team from outside the British Isles, then beat them 7-1 in Budapest the following year. That second scoreline is still England's heaviest defeat.

They lost the 1954 World Cup final to West Germany, a side they had already beaten 8-3 in the group stage. That single result is the entire reason they are not automatically first on lists like this one, which is precisely the flaw in ranking by trophies. Ninety minutes in Bern outweighs six years of evidence only if you think knockout football is a reliable measuring instrument. It is not.

Nándor Hidegkuti's deep-lying centre-forward role is the direct ancestor of the modern false nine, and defenders in 1953 had no framework for handling a striker who kept vacating the space they were trained to guard. [Why Hidegkuti was so hard to mark, and what the possession statistics miss about that side](/chapters/football/the-hungary-side-of-the-1950s-the-greatest-team-to-never-win-a-world-cup), explains the mechanism that broke England twice.

## Spain 2008-2012: the only side to win three consecutive major tournaments

Euro 2008, the 2010 World Cup, Euro 2012. No other national team has done it, and the 4-0 win over Italy in the Euro 2012 final is the most one-sided major final of the modern era.

Spain rank high on durability and influence and lower on margin. They won the 2010 World Cup scoring eight goals in seven matches, winning four knockout ties 1-0. That is control rather than dominance, and control produces narrow scorelines that understate how little the opposition was allowed to do.

Their weakness turned out to be structural rather than a matter of decline. Possession dominance depends on winning the ball back quickly and high, and a team willing to concede possession entirely and attack the space behind can break it. [What actually killed tiki-taka, and whether Spain's current game is better](/chapters/football/what-killed-tiki-taka-and-whether-spain-s-new-game-is-actually-better), traces the specific flaw that was exposed twice before anyone admitted it was a flaw.

## Yugoslavia 1987-1992: the counterfactual everyone gets wrong

The standard story is that war robbed a generation of certain glory. Yugoslavia won the 1987 World Youth Championship, produced Stojković, Savićević, Prosinečki, Mihajlović, Boban and Šuker, and were banned from Euro 92 by UN sanctions. Denmark took their place and won the tournament.

The standard story is too generous. A squad list is not a team, and the evidence that this group had solved its structural problems is thinner than the romance suggests. [The case that the decisive player in that generation is one nobody names](/chapters/football/the-yugoslavia-golden-generation-the-football-dynasty-that-died-with-a-country), works through what the side actually produced rather than what its talent implied it should have.

## Iceland 2016: the largest gap between resources and result

Iceland do not belong on a list of the best teams. They belong on this list because margin over the field has to account for the field a team was expected to occupy, and a country of roughly 330,000 people knocking England out of a European Championship is the largest overachievement in the sport's modern history.

It also was not luck, which is the part that gets misremembered. It was a deep block, a specific long-throw routine, and a captain nobody outside Iceland could name. [The tactical breakdown of how Iceland beat England](/chapters/football/iceland-at-euro-2016-a-tactical-breakdown-of-the-greatest-upset-in-modern-football), shows a plan detailed enough that calling it an upset undersells the coaching.

## The tactical ideas the great teams were built on

Every side above won by solving a spatial problem before their opponents understood it existed. Those solutions became the standard vocabulary of the sport, and each one is worth understanding on its own terms.

- [Guardiola's positional play](/chapters/football/guardiola-s-positional-play-why-it-looks-like-chaos-until-you-see-the-pattern) is the modern descendant of what Zagallo's Brazil did by instinct.
- [The half-space](/chapters/football/the-half-space-the-10-metres-of-grass-that-changed-how-football-is-played), the ten metres between the touchline and the centre, is where most of that positional theory is actually applied.
- [Gegenpressing](/chapters/football/gegenpressing-explained-why-every-team-copies-it-and-most-get-it-wrong) is the counter-idea that beat possession football, and most teams that copy it get it wrong in the same way.
- [Whether high pressing actually wins trophies](/chapters/football/does-high-pressing-actually-win-trophies-ten-years-of-ppda-data-have-an-answer), tested against ten years of PPDA data, is less settled than the coaching consensus suggests.
- [The high defensive line](/chapters/football/the-high-defensive-line-the-riskiest-bet-in-modern-football-and-why-teams-keep-taking-it) is the bet that makes pressing possible and the flaw that killed Spain's version of it.
- [The false nine](/chapters/football/the-false-9-is-dead-here-is-what-killed-it-and-what-replaced-it), Hidegkuti's invention, has been declared dead, and what replaced it is more interesting than the obituary.
- [The 4-3-3](/chapters/football/the-4-3-3-keeps-dying-and-coming-back-here-is-why-no-formation-can-kill-it) keeps being buried and keeps returning, for a reason that says something about all formations.
- [How Real Madrid win Champions League ties on the counter-attack](/chapters/football/how-real-madrid-win-the-champions-league-with-counter-attack-when-they-are-supposed-to-possess), while being built as a possession side, is the clearest modern example of a team winning by abandoning its own identity at the right moment.

## What the crowd contributes, measured rather than assumed

Home advantage is usually treated as atmosphere and left unquantified, which is lazy given that two of football's most famous crowds have been studied properly.

[Galatasaray's use of stadium noise as a deliberate tactical weapon](/chapters/football/how-galatasaray-turned-stadium-noise-into-a-tactical-weapon-and-the-science-behind-it) has measurable effects on visiting players' decision-making. [Dortmund's Südtribüne, the Yellow Wall](/chapters/football/the-yellow-wall-why-dortmund-s-s-dtrib-ne-is-the-most-studied-crowd-in-football), is the most studied crowd in the sport, and what researchers found is more specific than louder equals better.

There is also the question of why any of it produces such intense allegiance in people with no geographic connection to the club or country at all. [Bangladesh's obsession with Brazil and Argentina](/chapters/football/why-we-support-countries-we-never-lived-in) is the sharpest available case study.

## Which team at World Cup 2026 could join this list?

Probably none, and the reason is structural. The 2026 tournament expands to 48 teams across the United States, Canada and Mexico, with a Round of 32 adding a match. More games against weaker opposition compresses the margin a great side can generate, because the ceiling on beating a poor team is capped by how many goals anyone can realistically score.

The candidates worth watching are narrower than the pre-tournament noise will suggest. [Spain's tactical blueprint after Euro 2024](/chapters/football/spain-at-world-cup-2026-the-tactical-blueprint-after-winning-euro-2024) is the most coherent system going into the tournament. [The USA's home-advantage case rests on a profile question about Tyler Adams rather than Christian Pulisic](/chapters/football/can-the-usa-actually-use-home-advantage-at-world-cup-2026-their-tactical-profile-says-maybe), which is not how the tournament will be sold to American audiences.

Two things will decide more than tactics. [Why world-class players disappear at World Cups](/chapters/football/why-world-class-players-disappear-in-world-cups-the-psychology-behind-tournament-pressure) is a psychological pattern reliable enough to predict against, and 2026 is [the first World Cup of the era after Messi and Ronaldo](/chapters/football/what-football-looks-like-after-messi-and-ronaldo-are-gone), which removes the individual gravity that has shaped every tournament since 2006.

## The ranking

1. Brazil 1970. The widest margin ever produced at a World Cup, against the strongest available knockout field, with tactics a decade ahead of the language for them.
2. Hungary 1950-1954. Four years without a defeat, England destroyed twice, and one bad afternoon in Bern that cost them the trophy and their place at the top of most lists.
3. Spain 2008-2012. Unmatched on durability with three consecutive major tournaments, lower on margin because control produces 1-0 wins.
4. Yugoslavia 1987-1992. The highest ceiling never tested, ranked on evidence rather than on what the squad list implies.
5. Iceland 2016. Not among the best teams, and the largest gap between resources and result the sport has recorded.

What keeps pulling at me is not the order but the company in it. Two of the top four won nothing that the ranking depends on. That is about as direct an argument as I can make that the trophy cabinet is the worst available instrument for measuring how good a football team actually was.
