Every argument about the greatest football team ever collapses into the same problem within about four minutes. Someone says Brazil 1970. Someone says the eras cannot be compared. Someone mentions that today's average full-back is fitter than 1970's best forward, and the conversation dies without resolving anything.

The comparison problem is real, but it has a solution most lists refuse to use. Stop comparing teams across eras. Compare each team to the field it actually played, then rank the size of the gap. A side that beat its contemporaries by a wider margin than any other side beat theirs has the strongest claim, whatever decade the boots came from.

Ranked that way the order shifts, some famous teams fall, and a team that won nothing at all climbs into the top three.

## What actually makes a football team the greatest?

Four things, and I weight them in this order: margin over the field the team actually played, tactical originality, durability across years, and influence on the sport. Trophies are not one of them, and that exclusion is deliberate.

Margin over the field comes first. Goal difference, win rate, and the calibre of the opponents beaten. A dominant record against weak opposition is worth less than a narrow one against strong opposition, so the raw numbers have to be adjusted before they mean anything.

Then tactical originality. Did the team solve something nobody else had solved yet? Copying an idea well is a lesser achievement than inventing it, because the inventor was beating opponents who had no prepared answer.

Durability matters nearly as much. One tournament is a small sample, and football is noisy enough that a good side gets hot for six matches maybe once a decade. Sustained dominance across years is much harder to fake.

Influence comes last, and it is the softest of the four. It asks whether the sport reorganised itself around what the team did. I keep it in because it separates teams that won from teams that changed what winning looks like, but I do not let it outvote the evidence.

What is missing from that list is trophies as a standalone number. Trophies measure outcomes at the end of knockout tournaments, and knockout football is close to a coin flip between evenly matched sides. A team can be the best in the world for four years and win nothing, which is exactly what happened to two of the sides below.

## Was Brazil 1970 the greatest World Cup team ever?

On margin, yes. Six matches, six wins, 19 goals scored and seven conceded, with England, Uruguay and Italy all beaten on the way to a 4-1 win in the final — no other World Cup winner has paired a perfect record with that scoring rate against that quality of opposition.

The numbers hold up under inspection. Brazil averaged 3.17 goals a game across the tournament, and their 19 goals remain the second-highest total by any World Cup winner, behind West Germany's 25 in 1954, per [FIFA's statistical record of the 1970 squad](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/articles/brazil-selecao-pele-mexico-1970-statistics-stats). Pelé scored four and assisted six, meaning he was directly involved in more than half of everything Brazil produced. Jairzinho scored in all six matches, which had never been done before and has not been done since. [FIFA's Mexico 1970 tournament archive](https://www.fifa.com/tournaments/mens/worldcup/1970mexico) has the full match-by-match record if you want to check the schedule against the opposition quality yourself.

The tactical case is stronger than the nostalgia case. Mário Zagallo was running fluid positional rotation a year before Rinus Michels codified Total Football at Ajax, and doing it without the vocabulary that would later be invented to describe it. The team shifted between 4-2-4, 4-3-3 and 3-4-3 by phase of possession without a coach signalling any of it.

The honest counterargument is that the 1970 tournament had 16 teams and 32 matches in total, and Brazil played six of them, none going to extra time. Their physical reserves were never tested the way a modern side's are. That weakens the record without breaking it. [The full case for and against Brazil 1970, including where modern pressing benchmarks expose them](/chapters/football/was-the-1970-brazil-side-actually-the-greatest-team-ever-assembled-the-data-says-yes-but), is worth reading before you accept or dismiss the verdict, because the weakness in that side is specific and it is not the one people usually name.

## Who is the greatest football team never to win a World Cup?

Hungary's Aranycsapat, and it is not close. Between June 1950 and July 1954 they played 50 internationals for 42 wins, seven draws and one defeat, and the one defeat was the World Cup final itself.

They beat England 6-3 at Wembley in 1953, the first time England had ever lost at home to a team from outside the British Isles, then beat them 7-1 in Budapest the following year. That second scoreline is still England's heaviest defeat. [RSSSF's complete log of Hungary's internationals](https://www.rsssf.org/tablesh/hong-intres.html) carries the run match by match, which is the only way to see how relentless the fixture list actually was.

At the 1954 World Cup itself they scored 27 goals in five matches, still the most any team has scored at a single World Cup tournament. They lost the final to West Germany, a side they had already beaten 8-3 in the group stage. That single result is the entire reason they are not automatically first on lists like this one, which is precisely the flaw in ranking by trophies. Ninety minutes in Bern outweighs four years of evidence only if you think knockout football is a reliable measuring instrument. It is not.

Nándor Hidegkuti's deep-lying centre-forward role is the direct ancestor of the modern false nine, and defenders in 1953 had no framework for handling a striker who kept vacating the space they were trained to guard. [Why Hidegkuti was so hard to mark, and what the possession statistics miss about that side](/chapters/football/the-hungary-side-of-the-1950s-the-greatest-team-to-never-win-a-world-cup), explains the mechanism that broke England twice.

## Was Spain 2008-2012 the most dominant international team ever?

On durability, yes: Euro 2008, the 2010 World Cup and Euro 2012 make them the only national side to win three consecutive major tournaments. On margin, no, and the gap between those two answers is the whole story of that team.

Spain won the 2010 World Cup scoring eight goals in seven matches and conceding two, the lowest goal tally any World Cup winner has managed, with four knockout ties won 1-0. At Euro 2012 they conceded once in six matches and then won [the final against Italy 4-0](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/match/2003351--spain-vs-italy/), still the largest winning margin in the history of a European Championship final. That is control rather than dominance, and control produces narrow scorelines that understate how little the opposition was allowed to do. A team that wins by one goal four times running is not less dominant than one that wins by three; it is dominant in a way the scoreboard was never designed to record.

Their weakness turned out to be structural rather than a matter of decline. Possession dominance depends on winning the ball back quickly and high, and a team willing to concede possession entirely and attack the space behind can break it. [What actually killed tiki-taka, and whether Spain's current game is better](/chapters/football/what-killed-tiki-taka-and-whether-spain-s-new-game-is-actually-better), traces the specific flaw that was exposed twice before anyone admitted it was a flaw.

## How good was Yugoslavia's golden generation actually?

Good enough to win the 1987 World Youth Championship and reach the 1990 World Cup quarter-finals, and untested beyond that. The counterfactual that they would have won Euro 92 rests on a squad list rather than on anything the team produced.

The standard story is that war robbed a generation of certain glory. Yugoslavia produced Stojković, Savićević, Prosinečki, Mihajlović, Boban and Šuker, watched Red Star Belgrade win the 1991 European Cup, then were removed from Euro 92 by UN sanctions in May 1992. Denmark took their place and won the tournament.

The standard story is too generous. At Italia 90 they went out to Argentina on penalties after playing most of the tie a man down, which is the kind of result a very good side produces and a great one usually does not. A squad list is not a team, and the evidence that this group had solved its structural problems is thinner than the romance suggests. [The case that the decisive player in that generation is one nobody names](/chapters/football/the-yugoslavia-golden-generation-the-football-dynasty-that-died-with-a-country), works through what the side actually produced rather than what its talent implied it should have.

## How did Iceland beat England at Euro 2016?

With a deep, narrow two-bank block, a rehearsed long-throw routine, and two goals inside the opening 18 minutes: Ragnar Sigurðsson's equaliser and Kolbeinn Sigþórsson's winner in Nice. Every goal in the match was scored before the 19th minute, which is what a prepared plan looks like when it works.

Iceland do not belong on a list of the best teams. They belong on this list because margin over the field has to account for the field a team was expected to occupy, and a country whose total population was 332,529 on 1 January 2016 — [Statistics Iceland's official count](https://www.statice.is/publications/news-archive/inhabitants/population-1-january-2016/), smaller than Coventry — knocking England out of a European Championship is the largest overachievement in the sport's modern history.

It also was not luck, which is the part that gets misremembered. [UEFA's official match statistics](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/match/2018003--england-vs-iceland/statistics/) show England with the ball and the territory for most of the night and nothing to show for either, which is precisely what the plan was designed to produce. [The tactical breakdown of how Iceland beat England](/chapters/football/iceland-at-euro-2016-a-tactical-breakdown-of-the-greatest-upset-in-modern-football), shows a plan detailed enough that calling it an upset undersells the coaching.

## What tactical ideas were the greatest teams built on?

Every side above won by solving a spatial problem before their opponents understood it existed. Those solutions became the standard vocabulary of the sport, and each one is worth understanding on its own terms.

- [Guardiola's positional play](/chapters/football/guardiola-s-positional-play-why-it-looks-like-chaos-until-you-see-the-pattern) is the modern descendant of what Zagallo's Brazil did by instinct.
- [The half-space](/chapters/football/the-half-space-the-10-metres-of-grass-that-changed-how-football-is-played), the ten metres between the touchline and the centre, is where most of that positional theory is actually applied.
- [Gegenpressing](/chapters/football/gegenpressing-explained-why-every-team-copies-it-and-most-get-it-wrong) is the counter-idea that beat possession football, and most teams that copy it get it wrong in the same way.
- [Whether high pressing actually wins trophies](/chapters/football/does-high-pressing-actually-win-trophies-ten-years-of-ppda-data-have-an-answer), tested against ten years of PPDA data, is less settled than the coaching consensus suggests.
- [The high defensive line](/chapters/football/the-high-defensive-line-the-riskiest-bet-in-modern-football-and-why-teams-keep-taking-it) is the bet that makes pressing possible and the flaw that killed Spain's version of it.
- [The false nine](/chapters/football/the-false-9-is-dead-here-is-what-killed-it-and-what-replaced-it), Hidegkuti's invention, has been declared dead, and what replaced it is more interesting than the obituary.
- [The 4-3-3](/chapters/football/the-4-3-3-keeps-dying-and-coming-back-here-is-why-no-formation-can-kill-it) keeps being buried and keeps returning, for a reason that says something about all formations.
- [How Real Madrid win Champions League ties on the counter-attack](/chapters/football/how-real-madrid-win-the-champions-league-with-counter-attack-when-they-are-supposed-to-possess), while being built as a possession side, is the clearest modern example of a team winning by abandoning its own identity at the right moment.
- [The back three, and why the wing-backs matter more than the defenders](/chapters/football/the-back-three-revolution-how-wing-backs-became-the-most-important-position-in-modern-football) is the shape most modern sides now default to, and the consensus gets the important position wrong.
- [Breaking down a low block](/chapters/football/how-do-you-break-down-a-low-block-the-data-behind-football-s-hardest-problem) is the hardest problem in the sport, and the answer has less to do with crossing than with who moves before the ball arrives.

## Does the crowd actually change how a match is played?

Yes, and the effect is specific rather than atmospheric: crowds degrade visiting players' decision-making under noise and shift how referees award marginal decisions. Home advantage is usually left unquantified, which is lazy given that two of football's most famous crowds have been studied properly.

[Galatasaray's use of stadium noise as a deliberate tactical weapon](/chapters/football/how-galatasaray-turned-stadium-noise-into-a-tactical-weapon-and-the-science-behind-it) has measurable effects on visiting players' decision-making. [Dortmund's Südtribüne, the Yellow Wall](/chapters/football/the-yellow-wall-why-dortmund-s-s-dtrib-ne-is-the-most-studied-crowd-in-football), is the most studied crowd in the sport, and what researchers found is more specific than louder equals better.

The same effect scales up to whole continents. [South American ultras and barra bravas measurably change how referees officiate](/chapters/football/south-american-ultras-the-culture-behind-the-most-intimidating-atmospheres-in-world-football), and [African support turned a Qatar quarter-final into a de facto home game for Morocco](/chapters/football/african-football-culture-at-world-cup-2026-why-the-continent-s-support-changes-the-tournament), which is the closest thing to a controlled experiment on what a crowd is worth.

There is also the question of why any of it produces such intense allegiance in people with no geographic connection to the club or country at all. [Bangladesh's obsession with Brazil and Argentina](/chapters/football/why-we-support-countries-we-never-lived-in) is the sharpest available case study.

## Which team at World Cup 2026 could join this list?

Probably none, and the reason is the format rather than the players. The 2026 tournament runs 48 teams through 104 matches across the United States, Canada and Mexico, and a field that size mathematically compresses the margin any great side can generate.

More games against weaker opposition dilutes the record, because the ceiling on beating a poor team is capped by how many goals anyone can realistically score, while the added Round of 32 gives every contender one more chance to lose a coin flip. Brazil in 1970 needed six matches to be perfect; a 2026 winner needs eight.

The candidates worth watching are narrower than the pre-tournament noise will suggest. [Spain's tactical blueprint after Euro 2024](/chapters/football/spain-at-world-cup-2026-the-tactical-blueprint-after-winning-euro-2024) is the most coherent system going into the tournament. [The USA's home-advantage case rests on a profile question about Tyler Adams rather than Christian Pulisic](/chapters/football/can-the-usa-actually-use-home-advantage-at-world-cup-2026-their-tactical-profile-says-maybe), which is not how the tournament will be sold to American audiences.

Two things will decide more than tactics. [Why world-class players disappear at World Cups](/chapters/football/why-world-class-players-disappear-in-world-cups-the-psychology-behind-tournament-pressure) is a psychological pattern reliable enough to predict against, and 2026 is [the first World Cup of the era after Messi and Ronaldo](/chapters/football/what-football-looks-like-after-messi-and-ronaldo-are-gone), which removes the individual gravity that has shaped every tournament since 2006.

A knockout format also rewards the specific ability to recover from a losing position, which turns out to be less about character than the phrase suggests. [Which teams come back from two goals down, and why](/chapters/football/the-psychology-of-a-comeback-which-teams-come-back-from-two-goals-down-and-why) is the closest thing to a measurable answer. And when recovery fails, the tournament is decided by [penalty shootouts, which are not random](/chapters/football/penalty-shootouts-are-not-random-the-psychology-data-from-500-shootouts-proves-it): kick order, player status and preparation predict them well enough that treating shootouts as a lottery is a coaching error.

Germany are the clearest test of whether any of this is fixable by design. [Their rebuild was sold as the answer to 2018](/chapters/football/germany-s-tactical-rebuild-can-they-win-the-world-cup-eight-years-after-the-crash), and the exit suggests the original diagnosis was wrong.

## What is the final ranking of the greatest football teams?

Brazil 1970 first, Hungary 1950-1954 second, Spain 2008-2012 third, Yugoslavia 1987-1992 fourth and Iceland 2016 fifth — ordered by the gap between each side and the field it actually played, not by what ended up in the cabinet.

1. Brazil 1970. The widest margin ever produced at a World Cup, against the strongest available knockout field, with tactics a decade ahead of the language for them.
2. Hungary 1950-1954. Four years and one defeat in 50 internationals, England destroyed twice, and one bad afternoon in Bern that cost them the trophy and their place at the top of most lists.
3. Spain 2008-2012. Unmatched on durability with three consecutive major tournaments, lower on margin because control produces 1-0 wins.
4. Yugoslavia 1987-1992. The highest ceiling never tested, ranked on evidence rather than on what the squad list implies.
5. Iceland 2016. Not among the best teams, and the largest gap between resources and result the sport has recorded.

What keeps pulling at me is not the order but the company in it. Two of the top four won nothing that the ranking depends on. That is about as direct an argument as I can make that the trophy cabinet is the worst available instrument for measuring how good a football team actually was.
