In the 57th minute of the Euro 2004 final at the Estádio da Luz, Angelos Basinas swung a corner in from the right. Angelos Charisteas got above Costinha, and Greece led Portugal, the hosts, 1-0. The score stayed that way. A country that had never won a match at a major tournament before 12 June 2004 was champion of Europe 22 days later.

Everyone remembers the header. Hardly anyone remembers the Greek defender 40 yards behind it, standing with nobody to mark.

That was Traianos Dellas, and his job explains Euro 2004 better than anything else. Otto Rehhagel won with a role elite football had spent 15 years throwing out: the free defender, the spare man, the libero. A 150/1 outsider beat the hosts twice, the holders once and the tournament's best attacking side in a semi-final. If you want to understand how, start with Dellas and work outward. I don't think there's another way in.

## How unlikely was Greece winning Euro 2004?

No winner of a major men's international tournament has been less likely. UEFA's own account of the final has Greece starting as 80/1 outsiders, and some bookmakers went as long as 150/1. Across Euro 1980 and the 1994 World Cup, Greece had played six tournament matches and won none of them.

The record says more than the odds do. At USA 94 they lost all three group games without scoring. Rehhagel arrived in 2001 and began qualifying for Portugal with two 2-0 defeats, to Spain and Ukraine. Greece then won their next six qualifiers without conceding, including 1-0 against Spain in Zaragoza. That run was the first sign of what was coming. This team couldn't outplay anybody, but it could stop almost anybody from scoring.

## Why did Otto Rehhagel use a sweeper when nobody else did?

His players couldn't defend zonally against elite movement, but they could win one-on-one duels. A spare man behind them covered every duel they lost.

The history shows how out of step that was. For decades the libero sat at the centre of defensive thinking. Helenio Herrera's Inter won two European Cups in the 1960s with a sweeper behind man-markers, and Franz Beckenbauer made the position a playmaking role for Bayern Munich and West Germany in the 1970s. Then came Arrigo Sacchi's AC Milan, European champions in 1989 and 1990. Their flat back four moved as one zonal unit, squeezed the pitch and played offside better than any marker-and-sweeper system could. By 2004 nearly every serious side at the Euros defended zonally with four across the back. The libero had become shorthand for football that had fallen behind.

Rehhagel had come up through the Bundesliga of the 1970s and 1980s, and fashion didn't interest him much. In 1998 he won the league with newly promoted Kaiserslautern. [Sky Sports](https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11712/10260273/the-german-leicester) notes that no other promoted side has ever done it. He had already built one champion out of players nobody expected anything from. With Greece, the method was easy to describe and miserable to play against. Every defender got a specific opponent. One defender got nobody.

## What did Traianos Dellas actually do at Euro 2004?

Dellas was the free centre-back. He had no marking assignment, so he read where the danger was coming from and covered whichever marker had been beaten. When Greece had the ball, he could step up into midfield. Every other defender's mistake landed on him, and for six matches he cleaned them up.

Man-marking breaks down the same way almost every time. A clever forward drags his marker out of position and a teammate runs into the space. A zonal defence handles that by passing runners from one defender to the next, and Greece couldn't do that reliably. So they needed one player whose only job was to stand where the gap was about to open. Kapsis could follow a striker into the channel and Seitaridis could chase a winger inside, because Dellas was behind both of them.

The role suited him perfectly. Dellas was 6ft 5in and had spent the season at Roma. He read the game well and won nearly everything in the air, but he was slow on the turn. In a zonal back four, quick forwards would have found that out within minutes. As the spare man, he never had to turn and race anyone.

The system also gave Greece's best players jobs that suited them. Theodoros Zagorakis, the captain and UEFA's player of the tournament, could press and carry the ball out of midfield instead of guarding space. Zagorakis took the award, Charisteas took the goals and Rehhagel took the credit. None of it happens without Dellas free behind them.

## Which matches forced Greece to adapt?

Every round asked a different attacking question. Rehhagel changed the marking assignments each time, and he never moved the spare man.

### Portugal 1-2 Greece: 12 June, Estádio do Dragão, Porto

This was the opening game, and it gave Greece their first win at a major tournament. Giorgos Karagounis scored in the 7th minute, jumping on a loose Paulo Ferreira clearance and driving it low from 25 metres. Basinas made it 2-0 from the spot in the 51st minute after a foul by a 19-year-old Cristiano Ronaldo. Ronaldo's stoppage-time header was never more than a consolation ([UEFA match page](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/match/73736--portugal-vs-greece/)). Even that night you could see the pattern: once Portugal's wingers got past the first defender, they had nowhere to go.

### Greece 1-1 Spain: 16 June, Estádio do Bessa, Porto

Fernando Morientes put Spain ahead in the 28th minute, and after that the marking held. Michalis Kapsis stayed on Morientes all game with Dellas free behind him, and Charisteas equalised in the 66th minute. It looked like an ordinary draw at the time. As it turned out, this was the result that got Greece out of the group.

### Russia 2-1 Greece: 20 June, Estádio Algarve, Faro

Dmitri Kirichenko scored inside two minutes and Dmitri Bulykin added another in the 17th ([UEFA match page](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/match/73741--russia-vs-greece/)). Greece's only defeat of the tournament started in chaos, and they were two down before the markers had picked up their men. Zisis Vryzas pulled one back in the 43rd minute, and few consolation goals have ever been worth more. Greece and Spain finished level on four points with a drawn head-to-head and the same goal difference. Greece went through on goals scored, four to Spain's two.

### France 0-1 Greece: 25 June, Estádio José Alvalade, Lisbon

France were the holders, with Zidane, Henry and Trezeguet. Kapsis took Trezeguet. Giourkas Seitaridis followed Thierry Henry every time he drifted in from the left, and Dellas stayed free. Every route Henry tried was shut. In the 65th minute Zagorakis got past Bixente Lizarazu on the right and crossed for Charisteas to head in. [UEFA's report](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/history/news/0254-0d7b2525d006-ee6773aa3e1b-1000--slick-greece-shock-holders-france-in-euro-2004-quarter-f/) records it as Greece's first-ever win over France.

### Greece 1-0 Czech Republic: 1 July, Estádio do Dragão, Porto

The Czechs had been the best attacking side at the tournament. They lost Pavel Nedvěd in the first half after his knee took a knock in a collision with Kostas Katsouranis. In stoppage time at the end of the first period of extra time, Vassilios Tsiartas took a corner. Dellas, the man with nobody to mark, attacked the near post and headed it in. Under the silver goal rule, the team leading at the end of that first period won, so the goal ended the match. [UEFA](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/history/news/0253-0d811e88a032-1465bbb14f59-1000--euro-cult-heroes-traianos-dellas-2004) notes it was the last silver goal ever scored at a European Championship.

### Portugal 0-1 Greece: 4 July, Estádio da Luz, Lisbon

Portugal had eight shots on target to Greece's three and won 10 corners ([FBref match report](https://fbref.com/en/matches/e9f43994/Portugal-Greece-July-4-2004-European-Championship)). Greece needed only one of their own. [UEFA's final report](https://www.uefa.com/uefaeuro/history/news/0254-0d7b673abd33-d04e3bb2cd62-1000--greece-crowned-kings-of-europe-after-euro-2004-final-win-ag/) describes Ricardo coming off his line for Basinas's delivery, which left Charisteas an empty net. Figo and Ronaldo each had a Greek defender for company all evening, and Dellas swept up behind.

## Were Greece just lucky?

Lucky teams don't win three straight knockout matches without conceding. Greece went more than 280 minutes without letting in a goal against France, the Czech Republic and Portugal.

The luck argument at its strongest goes like this. Greece scored three knockout goals, all of them headers and two from corners, so a handful of set pieces decided the tournament. All of that is true, and it's why the result looks so unlikely on paper. But it mixes up where the goals came from with why Greece won. A set-piece team only wins if the opposition can't score from open play. In the knockout rounds Greece faced Henry, Trezeguet, Zidane, Baroš, Koller, Figo and Ronaldo, and none of them scored. A system can repeat a shutout like that. Chance doesn't give you three in a row.

The other objection is Denmark in 1992, who came in late to replace Yugoslavia and often get called the bigger shock. I don't buy it. Denmark had reached the semi-finals of Euro 1984, beaten Uruguay 6-1 at the 1986 World Cup and had Peter Schmeichel and Brian Laudrup in the side. They were a good team that got an unexpected invitation. Greece arrived in Portugal without a single tournament win in their history. They left having beaten the hosts twice and the holders once. No other nation has won a European Championship without ever winning a match at a major tournament beforehand.

## Why did the Greece model never work again?

It relied on surprise and on the right opponents. By 2008 Europe's best sides had worked out that constant movement from their forwards would pull the markers apart faster than one spare man could cover.

Greece went to Euro 2008 as holders and lost all three games, 2-0 to Sweden, 1-0 to Russia and 2-1 to Spain. No holder has ever defended the title worse. Spain went on to win that tournament, and they did the most damage to the old approach. Their midfielders kept swapping positions, which left every man-marker with two bad options: follow his player out of the defensive shape, or let him go. A spare man can clean up one mistake. He can't clean up after four players rotating at once.

Put simply, man-marking with a free defender works against teams that attack from fixed positions and struggles against teams that rotate. Greece mostly met the first kind in 2004. In 2008 they ran into the second.

## What does Greece 2004 mean for World Cup 2026?

Today's version of the idea is a compact low block with man-oriented duels inside it, and the 2026 format rewards it more than any World Cup before.

Morocco showed what that looks like at Qatar 2022. In their round-of-16 tie with Spain they had 23% of the ball. The match finished 0-0 and Morocco won the shootout 3-0 ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/soccer/report/_/gameId/633841)). Morocco defended in a narrow zonal shape rather than with Rehhagel's man-markers, but the principle was the same. They gave up possession and turned the game into a series of individual defensive battles they expected to win.

The [2026 World Cup format](https://www.fifa.com/en/articles/article-fifa-world-cup-2026-mexico-canada-usa-new-format-tournament-football-soccer) makes that approach more valuable. There are 48 teams, the eight best third-placed sides go through and there's a new round of 32, so the eventual champion plays eight matches. More of those games are knockout ties, and more of them can be settled by a single set piece. In 2004 Greece needed a goals-scored tiebreak just to get out of Group A. In 2026 a team like that has a cushion, because third place is often enough.

Man-oriented marking has come back in club football too. Gian Piero Gasperini built Atalanta's Europa League-winning side on a press where each player follows an opponent, and plenty of coaches have borrowed from it. The modern version happens much higher up the pitch, and far more aggressively, than anything Greece tried.

## What problem has the Greece model still not solved?

Scoring. Greece scored seven goals in six matches at Euro 2004, and the three that won knockout ties were all headers. Morocco played 120 minutes against Spain without a goal. Defensive underdogs have become very good at not losing, but they still lack a repeatable way to win. That's why so many of their runs come down to corners and shootouts.

Until someone pairs Dellas's free role with a real way of scoring from open play, underdogs will keep reaching semi-finals and titles like 2004 will stay rare. Rehhagel never had to solve that, because three headers were enough. The World Cup now has 48 teams and eight matches between a team and the trophy. Whichever coach solves it could change who wins these tournaments.
