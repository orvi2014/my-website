It is the 67th minute at El Molinón in Gijón, 16 June 1982, and Karl-Heinz Rummenigge has just made it 1-1. He got across his marker and stabbed in a low cross from Felix Magath, which is impressive for a man carrying a muscle injury who can barely run. The 42,000 inside the ground think they know what comes next. West Germany are European champions. Algeria had never played a World Cup match before that afternoon. The Germans assume this equaliser is the first of several.

Algeria kick off. Nine passes later Salah Assad is flying down the left on the overlap. He whips it across the face of goal, and at the far post is Lakhdar Belloumi, the reigning African Footballer of the Year. 2-1, 68th minute. West Germany don't score again.

That's the version most people know: a lovely goal, a stunned giant, and a scandal eight days later. It leaves out the part I find most interesting. To see it, you have to look at what the numbers from that afternoon say and then work out why they're wrong.

## How did Algeria beat West Germany in 1982?

**Algeria won 2-1 by defending deep on purpose, absorbing German pressure and breaking quickly into the space Germany's attackers left behind. Rabah Madjer (54') and Lakhdar Belloumi (68') both scored from fast, direct moves rather than long spells of possession.**

Nobody scored in the first half, but Algeria weren't hanging on. [FIFA's own retrospective](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/articles/algeria-west-germany-world-cup-upsets) says they more than matched the favourites before the break, playing quick one-touch football that kept Germany defending far more than they'd planned for. Mahieddine Khalef, who shared the bench with Rachid Mekhloufi, told his players at half-time to push higher still and go at the Germans with pace up front.

Nine minutes after the restart they scored. Algeria won the ball and went the length of the pitch. Harald Schumacher could only parry Belloumi's shot and Madjer poked in the rebound. The move took seconds, and Germany's midfield was nowhere near back in shape when it finished.

The defending was just as carefully planned. Noureddine Kourichi was given Horst Hrubesch, the big centre-forward Germany played through, and he left him almost nothing on the ground or in the air. Mehdi Cerbah, one of the shortest players on the pitch, cleaned up whatever got past. Chaabane Merzekane kept running beyond the ball from right-back, and late on only Schumacher stopped him scoring a third. On ITV, Hugh Johns called him "one of the discoveries of the World Cup."

Mustapha Dahleb ran the middle of the pitch for Algeria and played his club football at Paris Saint-Germain. [He told The Set Pieces](https://thesetpieces.com/latest-posts/world-cup-1982-algeria-2-west-germany-1/) what the plan was. The Germans "wanted to tire us, they felt we would fold physically... we were just waiting for them, ready to pounce on the break."

## What did the statistics from Algeria vs West Germany show?

**On paper, West Germany were in charge. They won 16 corners to Algeria's 4 and had most of the territory. They still lost, because Algeria had decided to give them that territory.**

The corner count comes from [These Football Times' account of the match](https://thesefootballtimes.co/2019/05/16/we-will-dedicate-our-seventh-goal-to-our-wives-the-complacency-that-gifted-algeria-a-legendary-win-over-west-germany/). It's the only volume stat most retellings bother to keep, and a modern model would love it. Sixteen corners reads as constant pressure, a steady supply of balls into the box and an opponent stuck in its own half. Put the game into a 2026 dashboard and you'd probably see German possession well north of 60%, a big territorial edge, a high Algerian PPDA because they hardly pressed in Germany's half, and an xG total leaning towards the side taking all the corners.

None of those figures would be inaccurate. Read together, they'd still tell you the wrong team was in control.

## What can xG and possession not measure about Algeria's 1982 team?

**Intent. Event data can't separate a team being pinned back from a team that has picked where the game gets played, and Algeria in 1982 were doing the picking.**

I keep coming back to this, so I'll give it a name: consented territory. In the event stream, a deep block and a team that's falling apart look the same. Both concede corners. Both let the other side have the ball. Both post a high PPDA because neither presses high. The difference is whether the space in front of their box was handed over as part of a plan, with a trigger for going the other way, or taken off them.

Gijón gave us the test in the 68th minute. A team that's genuinely pinned, and has just watched its lead cancelled out by one of the best forwards in the world, doesn't put nine passes together and score inside a minute. It panics, or it hoofs it clear. Algeria went straight back to the move they'd been waiting on all afternoon. The equaliser changed the score. It didn't change what they were trying to do.

Possession and corners record what happened. They don't record whether the side without the ball was losing its shape or setting a trap. For that you need the transitions: how many Algerians were already on the move before the ball was won, and how fast the full-backs got forward. A corner count won't show you any of it.

Germany did have information on Algeria, just not numbers. Jupp Derwall had video of them and, [by his own later admission](https://thesefootballtimes.co/2019/05/16/we-will-dedicate-our-seventh-goal-to-our-wives-the-complacency-that-gifted-algeria-a-legendary-win-over-west-germany/), didn't show it to his players because he thought they'd laugh at him. Before the game he said he'd get the first train home to Munich if they lost. Paul Breitner is widely credited with the line about dedicating the seventh goal to the players' wives and the eighth to their dogs. The footage was there. Nobody watched it.

## Wasn't Algeria's win just German complacency and an injured Rummenigge?

**No. Four days later the same German side beat Chile 4-1, and they went on to reach the World Cup final. Algeria's approach also worked against other teams, before Gijón and after it.**

Half of this argument is right, which is why it deserves a proper answer. Germany were arrogant and Rummenigge was hurt. *Kicker* wrote afterwards that "in midfield, nothing at all happened." But complacency explains why Germany were caught cold. It doesn't explain two Algerian goals.

Look at what Germany did next. On 20 June, at the same ground, they beat Chile 4-1 with a Rummenigge hat-trick. They knocked out France on penalties in the semi-final in Seville and lost the final 3-1 to Italy. That isn't a side that was broken.

Nor was Algeria's win out of nowhere. On 1 January 1964 in Algiers, they beat West Germany 2-0 in a friendly. In March 1980 they reached the Africa Cup of Nations final and lost 3-0 to Nigeria in Lagos, and in 1982 they made the semi-finals again. Belloumi said those two tournaments gave the squad the international experience it took to Spain.

The best evidence is in Algeria's own group. On 21 June at the Estadio Carlos Tartiere in Oviedo, Austria beat them 2-0 through Walter Schachner and Hans Krankl. Austria's coach, Georg Schmidt, had been watching Algeria since the Africa Cup of Nations in Libya. So the one European side in the group that had done its homework on Algeria was the one that beat them. You don't need a scouting report to stop a fluke. You need one to stop a system.

Three days later, back in Oviedo, Algeria were 3-0 up against Chile inside 35 minutes, with two from Assad and one from Tedj Bensaoula. They held on to win 3-2.

## What was the Disgrace of Gijón?

**The Disgrace of Gijón was West Germany's 1-0 win over Austria on 25 June 1982. Hrubesch scored in the 10th minute, and after that neither side really attacked, because that scoreline put both through and eliminated Algeria.**

The fixture list allowed it. Algeria had played Chile the day before, so by the time West Germany and Austria kicked off, everyone knew a German win by one or two goals would send both European teams through. Group 2 ended with three teams on four points: West Germany (+3), Austria (+2) and Algeria (0).

[Opta Analyst's reconstruction](https://theanalyst.com/articles/disgrace-gijon-west-germany-austria-1982-world-cup-stats) shows how dead it was. There were two shots in the entire second half, the fewest in any World Cup second half in 796 matches since 1966, and none at all in the final 40 minutes. In the second half, 58% of passes stayed inside the teams' own halves, against a World Cup average of 41%. Only 0.9% of second-half touches were in the opposition box. Bob Valentine, the Scottish referee, said it took him about half an hour to realise what was going on. Benali Sekkal, president of the Algerian federation, called it "scandalous and immoral." FIFA threw out Algeria's complaint.

This time the numbers got it right. Event data picks up a cynical match straight away. What it missed was how clever the match eight days earlier had been, and that gap is what this piece is about.

## How did the 1982 upset change African football?

**FIFA started playing every final group match at the same time, and that rule is still in place. The win also chipped away at the idea that African teams went to World Cups to make up the numbers.**

Africa had two places at the 24-team tournament in 1982. Tunisia had won Africa's first World Cup match four years earlier, and Algeria's win over Germany was only the second. Cameroon, the other African side in Spain, drew with Peru, Poland and Italy and went home unbeaten. Algeria went out on goal difference after a match that neither team was trying to win. Neither debutant was knocked out by bad football.

Things did change after that. In 1986 Morocco won a group that included England, Poland and Portugal. In 1990 Cameroon beat Argentina, the holders, in the opening game in Milan and reached the quarter-finals. Morocco made the semi-finals in 2022, and CAF got nine direct places for 2026. Khalef said later that what made him proud was that after Gijón, "the Germans didn't look down on Africa as if there's no football being played there."

## Does the 1982 Algeria story matter for World Cup 2026?

**Yes. Algeria played Austria again on the last matchday of Group J at World Cup 2026, and the 48-team format brought back the same problem that made Gijón possible: teams knowing in advance which result suited them.**

I didn't expect the echo to be quite so exact. Algeria [qualified for 2026](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/algeria-qualify) under Vladimir Petković, lost 3-0 to Argentina at Arrowhead Stadium in Kansas City in their opener, then beat Jordan 2-1 in Santa Clara. On 27 June they were back in Kansas City to face Austria.

Both Group J games kicked off at the same time, as the post-Gijón rule requires. The catch is that simultaneous kick-offs only protect teams inside the same group. Eight third-placed sides qualify, several groups had already finished, and [ESPN reported](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/49203568/algeria-austria-iran-2026-world-cup-draw-advance-eliminated) that both teams knew before kick-off that a draw would take them both through. Iran were done and waiting, and would have gone through if either side had won. Forty-four years on, it was Gijón again with a different team left watching.

This time the game was nothing like 1982. Riyad Mahrez scored twice, the second in stoppage time, to make it 3-2 to Algeria. Moments later Saša Kalajdžić headed Austria level, according to [FIFA's match report](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/algeria-austria-match-report-highlights). It ended 3-3, both went through and Iran went home. Algeria's tournament finished on 3 July in Vancouver with a 2-0 loss to Switzerland, [as Opta Analyst detailed](https://theanalyst.com/articles/switzerland-vs-algeria-stats-world-cup-2026).

Gijón still teaches two things. One is about the rules. FIFA fixed the 1982 problem inside groups, but the bigger format has opened it up again across groups, and staggered kick-offs can't do anything about that. The other is about analysis, and it's the one I care about more. At this tournament, any team that sat deep against a better side ended up with the same possession and territory numbers as a team simply getting battered. You only know a deep block was a tactic when you see the transition it was built for, and an event log doesn't show you that.

Derwall had the tape and didn't use it. Today's version of the mistake is harder to spot: you look at the dashboard and assume you've seen the match. Belloumi's goal was nine passes long. It started in Algeria's own half, a minute after they'd lost their lead. The 16-4 corner count gave no warning of it, but anyone watching what Algeria's players were doing would have seen it coming.
