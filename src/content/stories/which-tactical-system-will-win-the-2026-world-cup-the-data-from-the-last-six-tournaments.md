The question in the headline got its answer on 19 July at MetLife Stadium, and a lot of people have misread it. Spain beat Argentina 1–0 after extra time with roughly two-thirds of the ball, and the verdict wrote itself. Positional play was back on top: keep the ball, press high, suffocate the opponent, lift the trophy.

Spain's own recent history argues otherwise. The same possession game produced 1,137 passes and a shootout defeat to Russia in 2018. Four years later it produced 77% of the ball and another shootout defeat, this time to Morocco. The style barely changed between Moscow, Doha and New Jersey. What changed was how little Spain gave away at the other end, and who Luis de la Fuente sent on after the hour.

I went back through the last six World Cups, 2006 to 2026, looking for the formation or style that separates champions from the rest. I didn't find one. What I found is duller than that, and a lot more useful if you're trying to call 2030.

## Does possession football win the World Cup?

Not on its own. The Spain teams that went home early had as much of the ball as the one that won, so possession can't be the thing that separated them.

2018 is the clearest example. Spain completed 1,029 of 1,137 passes against Russia in the round of 16, a World Cup record at the time, then drew 1–1 and lost 4–3 on penalties. [Yahoo Sports' match report](https://sports.yahoo.com/toothless-spain-breaks-passing-record-still-crashes-world-cup-russia-penalties-164423634.html) described a team that "passed its own World Cup campaign to death." Against Morocco in 2022, Spain had more than 75% of the ball over 120 minutes and managed one shot on target before the shootout.

Look at the other champions and it holds up. Italy won in 2006 on defensive organisation. France won in 2018 in a final where Croatia had more of the ball, at a tournament where [43% of all goals (73 of 169) came from set pieces](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/set-piece-goals-2018), the highest share since 1966. The winning goals in France's quarter-final and semi-final were both headers from dead balls. Argentina switched to a back five for their 2022 quarter-final against the Netherlands. That's six champions and at least four different ways of playing. All six started from a back four, and that's about as far as the formation pattern goes.

So neither formation nor possession share picks out the champion. You find the better clue at the other end of the pitch.

## Why did Spain actually win the 2026 World Cup?

Because opponents hardly ever got a look at goal. Spain allowed 2.3 expected goals across eight matches, around 0.3 a game, and [Opta Analyst](https://theanalyst.com/articles/spain-win-world-cup-final-2026-stats-analysis) says that's the lowest figure recorded by any team at a men's World Cup.

They conceded once all tournament, when Charles De Ketelaere equalised for Belgium in the 41st minute of the quarter-final. The previous best for a champion was two, shared by France in 1998, Italy in 2006 and Spain in 2010. A defence like that isn't compulsory, since Argentina let in eight on their way to the trophy in Qatar. It does make everything else easier. A team giving up 0.3 xG a game can go 85 minutes without scoring and still be in control of the match.

Spain used the ball to defend. In the semi-final, their press held France, the tournament's most-hyped attack, to two shots, neither on target, before Pedro Porro made it 2–0 in the 58th minute. [Opta's analysis of that game](https://theanalyst.com/articles/spains-intensity-crushes-hype-france-attack-world-cup-stats) found that Michael Olise lost possession 20 times in the first 70 minutes, more than in any of his previous six matches. In the final, Argentina became the first team to get through 90 minutes of a World Cup final without a shot. Spain's field tilt, their share of final-third passes, was 83.3%.

All of it was built around a 19-year-old. Pau Cubarsí played 750 minutes, the joint-most of any outfield player, and [Opta](https://theanalyst.com/articles/pau-cubarsi-world-cup-2026-young-player-of-the-tournament) had him on 671 completed passes. Only Rodri, with 756, completed more, and those are the two highest totals on record at a World Cup. If your centre-back and your holding midfielder are the team's two biggest passers, your possession starts deep, in areas where losing the ball costs you nothing. The 2022 side kept the ball too. The 2026 side kept it somewhere safe.

## Who was Spain's most important player at the 2026 World Cup?

Rodri took the Golden Ball and Cubarsí won Young Player of the Tournament. The player who decided Spain's route, though, was Mikel Merino, and he began both of his decisive knockout games on the bench.

Merino is 30 and plays for Arsenal. In September 2025 he scored a hat-trick in [Spain's 6–0 win away to Türkiye](https://www.aljazeera.com/sports/2025/9/7/spain-thump-turkiye-6-0-in-world-cup-2026-qualifier-as-merino-scores-three), the result that settled qualifying Group E. Spain finished that group with 16 points, 21 goals scored and two conceded. Even so, he didn't start a knockout game in North America.

He didn't have to. Against Portugal in Dallas on 6 July, with the game goalless in front of 70,649 people, two substitutes combined in the first minute of stoppage time: Ferran Torres set up Merino. Four days later against Belgium, Merino scored [one minute and 57 seconds after coming on](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/spain-belgium-match-report-highlights). It was the 88th minute, and Senne Lammens had spilled a Cubarsí shot into his path. Then Torres, another substitute, won the final in the 106th minute. [FIFA's review of the tournament](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/spain-champions-stats-statistics) counts three of Spain's five knockout matches as swung by players off the bench. Torres is only the second substitute to score the winner in a World Cup final, after Mario Götze in 2014.

Their club situations help explain why nobody built the coverage around them. By early September, Merino had played two Premier League matches and 35 minutes. Torres, 26, left Barcelona for Paris Saint-Germain in August for €50 million after scoring 21 goals in 49 games in 2025–26. Neither is a headline starter for club or country. De la Fuente picked both for a specific job, and that job was the last half-hour.

## Do substitutes decide World Cup knockout matches?

More often than you'd think. In five of the last six World Cups, a substitute made a decisive contribution in at least one of the champion's knockout games. No formation comes close to that kind of consistency.

Alessandro Del Piero came off the bench to score in the 121st minute of Italy's 2006 semi-final against Germany. Cesc Fàbregas came on in the 2010 final and set up Andrés Iniesta's 116th-minute winner. Götze came on in 2014 and scored in the 113th. Gonzalo Montiel came on in the 2022 final and scored the penalty that won the shootout. And in 2026, Merino and Torres produced three decisive contributions between them in 14 days.

France in 2018 are the exception, though they fit the broader point. Didier Deschamps' side leaned on set pieces, which is the other dependable way to win a tight knockout match you aren't dominating. Champions have a plan for scoring once open play dries up. Possession doesn't give you one. The most it does is get you to the 70th minute without conceding.

## Didn't Spain win because of Lamine Yamal and Rodri?

This is the best counter-argument, and it's half right. The trouble is that people remember the wrong half.

The starters built the platform. Rodri completed more passes and covered more ground than anyone at the tournament. Lamine Yamal won the penalty that Mikel Oyarzabal scored in the 22nd minute of the semi-final, and Oyarzabal had already scored twice in the 3–0 round-of-32 win over Austria. When a game opened up, the first XI finished it off: 4–0 against Saudi Arabia, 3–0 against Austria, 2–0 against France.

The games that stayed tight tell a different story. Spain opened the tournament with a 0–0 draw against Cape Verde, who were making their World Cup debut, despite outshooting them [27 to six](https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/13554452/spain-0-0-cape-verde-african-nation-defying-one-of-biggest-mismatches-in-football-history-shows-beauty-of-the-game). After that, every tight knockout game (1–0, 2–1, 1–0) was won by a substitute, in the 91st, 88th and 106th minutes. The best starting XI at the tournament couldn't break down a low block by itself. That was exactly the problem that sent Spain home in 2018 and 2022.

It's not a one-off, either. At Euro 2024, Merino came off the bench to head the 119th-minute winner against Germany in the quarter-final, and Oyarzabal came off the bench to score the 86th-minute winner against England in the final. Across Spain's last two tournament wins, substitutes have scored the winner in five knockout matches. You could put one of those down to luck, but five in two summers starts to look like a plan.

## How did the 48-team format and the North American hosts change what wins?

The expanded format added a round of 32, so the finalists played an eighth match. Add a North American summer, and the bench stops being a backup and becomes part of the plan from the first whistle.

Spain played in Atlanta, Guadalajara, Los Angeles, Dallas and New Jersey. Group H brought Cape Verde (0–0), Saudi Arabia (4–0) and Uruguay (1–0, in Guadalajara), then Austria in the round of 32 (3–0). That part of the draw was kind. Portugal, Belgium, France and Argentina in 14 days was the hardest run any 2026 finalist faced. Opta's supercomputer had Spain as [pre-tournament favourites at 16.1%](https://theanalyst.com/articles/who-will-win-2026-fifa-world-cup-predictions-opta-supercomputer), about one in six. That looks fair for a side that needed late winners in three of those four games.

You can see the load in the minutes. Cubarsí played 750 of a possible 780, and Rodri played in every match. The spine couldn't be rested, so De la Fuente spread the work across the front six and sent on Merino and Torres once opponents' legs had gone. The format didn't create that pattern, but it did reward the team already built for it. The spine had its limits too. A tired Rodri in the final 20 minutes was a real risk, so Martín Zubimendi replaced him midway through the first half of extra time, and the structure held.

The squad heads into 2026–27 in decent shape. After a 21-day break, Cubarsí has played 242 La Liga minutes for Barcelona with a 7.3 FotMob average. Rodri had a minor back procedure, planned in advance, and Manchester City say it won't affect his season. Pedro Porro, who turns 27 today and scored in both the round of 32 and the semi-final, is still Spain's attacking outlet from right-back.

## What tactical system will win the next World Cup?

The one that gives away the fewest chances and has the best plan for the last 30 minutes. Formation and possession share come a distant second.

Here's my view, plainly. A way of playing won't win you a World Cup. You win one by conceding almost nothing across eight games and having someone other than your starters to finish the tight ones. Possession helped Spain in 2026 because it was set up to protect Cubarsí and Rodri. The 2018 and 2022 teams had just as much of the ball without that protection, and they went home early.

If that's right, a few things follow. Teams that copy Spain's possession numbers without the rest defence will end up repeating Spain's 2022, with loads of the ball, one shot on target and a shootout. The 2030 champion will allow well under half an expected goal per game, because Spain have just shown how far that can take a team. And at least one of that champion's knockout games will be settled after the 75th minute by a substitute or a set piece.

Bookmakers have opened Spain as [favourites to retain the trophy at +450](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/49410201/spain-open-betting-favorite-repeat-2030-world-cup), roughly 18%, with co-hosts Portugal level with England at +900 and the United States and Mexico at 80-1. I'd have Spain a little lower, nearer 15%. They'll be co-hosts, and Yamal and Cubarsí will both be 23, which counts for a lot. But Rodri and Merino will be 34, nobody has won back-to-back World Cups since Brazil in 1962, and Spain will need another substitute as easy to overlook as Merino was.

Over the next four years, pay less attention to who De la Fuente starts and more to who he trusts in the 85th minute. That's where Spain won this World Cup.
