Go back and watch the first minute of Austria against Türkiye in Leipzig on 2 July 2024. Not the goal. The two seconds before it. Arda Güler stands over a corner on the right. Three Turkish players break for the near post at the same instant, and not one of them is going to attack the ball. They are there to stand in the space Patrick Pentz needs to walk into. That is the part of set-piece play the data captures badly and the eye captures worse. The decisive action happens before contact, and the players performing it never touch the ball.

Pentz came and got nowhere near it. The ball pinballed around the six-yard box until it dropped to Merih Demiral, who smashed it high past him. Fifty-seven seconds. Fastest goal in the history of the European Championship knockout stages. On the hour Güler delivered another one and Demiral headed the second. Türkiye won 2-1 having had six shots. Austria had 60 per cent of the ball, 21 shots and a flight home.

That is the match everyone points at when they say set pieces won Euro 2024. They are right about the outcome and wrong about the reason, and the difference matters for what comes next.

## How many goals at Euro 2024 actually came from set pieces?

Thirteen of the 117 goals scored across the 51 matches came from corners, 11.1 per cent, from 510 corners taken. Not one goal in the whole tournament came from a direct free kick.

Those numbers do not say what the post-tournament consensus said they say. Five hundred and ten corners produced 13 goals. One goal per 39 corners. A strike rate of 2.5 per cent. The idea that hardened over the summer of 2024, that the dead ball had become international football's decisive weapon and that tournament football had been solved by the corner flag, is not supported by its own evidence.

The club game is the useful comparison. In the 2025-26 Premier League season, 27.4 per cent of all goals came from non-penalty set pieces, the second-highest share in the competition's 34 seasons, and [goals from corners alone hit an all-time high of 18 per cent](https://theanalyst.com/articles/arsenal-most-goals-from-corners-premier-league-season-record), up from 12.1 per cent the year before.

So the tournament everyone called a set-piece festival produced corner goals at two-thirds the rate of the league it fed into 18 months later. Euro 2024 did not prove that set pieces are powerful. It proved that international sides are bad at them, and that being slightly less bad than the opposition is now enough to win a knockout tie.

Two rows carry the whole argument. The tournament-wide conversion figure of 2.5 per cent is the one that got quoted all summer, and it is the one that means the least. The spread underneath it, Georgia at one shot from 14 against Poland at seven from ten, is where the actual information lives.

## Is a corner really only worth 0.03 xG?

On the tournament-wide average, yes, and the average is close to useless. A corner's mean value really is about 0.03 goals, but that figure pools coached routines and hopeful ones into a single number that describes almost no individual team.

This is the analytics-era article of faith. The average corner is worth around 0.025 to 0.03 goals, so corners are noise and the training time is wasted. The number is real. The conclusion drawn from it is a statistical error, because that average is pooled across two populations that have almost nothing to do with each other.

Look at the spread inside Euro 2024's own corner data rather than the mean. Georgia took 14 corners across the tournament and generated exactly one shot from them. Poland got shots from seven of their ten. Same action, same laws of the game, a shot-generation rate that differs by an order of magnitude. Average those two together and you produce a number that describes neither of them. It is the football equivalent of reporting the mean income of a room containing a billionaire and a hundred students, then advising the students not to bother working.

The club evidence is blunter. In 2023-24, Arsenal scored 20 non-penalty set-piece goals in the Premier League, the most in the division, from 202 set-piece shots against a set-piece xG of 14.9. Fourth-most valuable chances in the league, most goals scored from them. That is what happens when the routine rather than the delivery is doing the work. An xG model prices a header from 12 yards without knowing that the two defenders who should have contested it were legally screened out of the picture a second earlier. By 2025-26, [Nicolas Jover's Arsenal had 25 set-piece goals and 19 from corners](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4187343), a Premier League record.

The average corner is worth 0.03 goals in roughly the way the average footballer is worth £8m. True, useless, and actively misleading if you plan around it. The distribution is bimodal. There are coached corners and uncoached corners, and treating them as one population is how a generation of analysts talked themselves out of the fastest-growing source of goals in the sport.

## Why did England beat Slovakia with a throw-in instead of a corner?

Because the corners were not rehearsed and the throw-in was. England took nine corners against Slovakia on 30 June 2024 and scored from none of them. They scored from a Kyle Walker long throw that Gareth Southgate later confirmed had been drilled specifically in training.

That match is the best piece of evidence in the tournament and almost nobody reads it correctly. [Opta had England on 63 per cent possession](https://theanalyst.com/articles/england-vs-slovakia-stats-euro-2024-live), 14 shots and 42 attacks into the box against Slovakia's 17, with nine corners to one. It was 1-0 Slovakia in the 95th minute. Then Walker hurled a throw into the area, Marc Guéhi got the faintest flick on it as a decoy rather than a finisher, and Jude Bellingham hooked in an overhead kick. Harry Kane headed the winner from Ivan Toney's flick-on 50 seconds into extra time. UEFA's own technical report rated Bellingham's the second-best goal of the tournament and picked up the same underlying trend: long throws spreading as a way to break a low block.

Then the embarrassing part. Before a ball was kicked at Euro 2024, Opta had already published the observation that no team had conceded more set-piece goals across the previous two European Championships than Slovakia, seven of the 13 they shipped. It was in the public stat packs. Fifty-four per cent of their tournament concessions came from dead balls. The flaw was documented, and it was still there four years later, when England, with a squad worth ten times as much, needed a rehearsed throw-in to rescue them in the 95th minute. Nobody fixed it. Nobody was made to.

Türkiye's 4-2-3-1 under Vincenzo Montella was built to sit off, concede territory and attack the transition and the dead ball, with Güler drifting in from the right as the designated deliverer and Demiral pushed up as a near-post attacker rather than a body to be marked. Ralf Rangnick's Austria were built for the opposite thing entirely: a high, aggressive press designed to win the ball in the final third and generate volume. Rangnick got his volume. Twenty-one shots. He also fielded a defensive set-piece structure that an inswinging ball and three players walking into a goalkeeper's path took apart twice in 59 minutes.

## How did Spain win Euro 2024 without set-piece goals?

Because they were the only side at the tournament who could break a low block in open play, so they never needed the dead ball as a rescue mechanism. It is the strongest objection to the set-piece case, and answering it takes real numbers.

Spain won all seven matches, [scored a record 15 goals, beating France's 14 from 1984](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/40562354/spain-european-championship-goals-record-title), and did it overwhelmingly in open play, with Nico Williams and Lamine Yamal pinned to the touchlines in a 4-3-3, Rodri as the single pivot, and a final in which Williams completed all 46 of his passes, scored and assisted.

Now run it the other way. Spain conceded four goals in seven matches and none of them came from a corner. Luis de la Fuente's side were the only team at Euro 2024 who were competent in both phases, which is exactly why they never needed the dead ball as a rescue mechanism. The 4-3-3 with two genuine touchline wingers was chosen because it stretches a low block horizontally and manufactures open-play chances against compact defences, which was the problem everyone else was solving with corners because they could not solve it with football. Spain are not the counterexample. They are the control group. One team out of 24 reached that standard, and that is the point.

Germany are the other side of the ledger, and they got there deliberately. Julian Nagelsmann's 4-2-3-1 had Mads Buttgereit working solely on set pieces, and Nagelsmann brought in Alfred Schreuder partly so Buttgereit could stay specialised. Germany's corner routines at Euro 2024 were the most varied in the tournament: short options, flat grounded deliveries into pre-agreed lanes, structures designed specifically against man-marking and zonal hybrids. That is what a coached corner looks like, and it is not the same event as the 510-corner average.

## Which formations actually make a team dangerous at set pieces?

Formations matter much less than staffing. Going into 2025-26, [ten of the Premier League's 20 clubs employed a dedicated set-play coach](https://www.creativesetplays.com/post/premier-league-set-piece-coaches-2025-26), in a competition where set pieces were about to produce 27.4 per cent of all goals.

As a hiring market, that is absurd. Half the clubs in the richest league in the world were not paying anyone to own a phase that generates more than a quarter of the goals. International football is worse, because the tournament head coach gets three weeks with a squad and the aerial routine is the one thing that transfers instantly. No chemistry required, no possession patterns to embed. Twelve players and a whiteboard. Türkiye, nobody's idea of a contender, reached a quarter-final on two inswinging corners from a teenager. Georgia took 14 corners and produced one shot. Portugal took 47, more than anyone, 9.4 a game, and went out on penalties to France.

## What does this mean for Euro 2028 and this season?

The teams that treat the dead ball as a coached phase rather than a hopeful one will keep converting at three to five times the tournament average, and the gap will widen, because the defensive side of it is still barely staffed. I am deliberately not leaning on this summer's World Cup numbers. I want audited event data before I build an argument on them, and the Euro 2024-to-Premier-League chain is the cleaner test anyway.

So here is the prediction I will be held to. The next major tournament will be decided by defensive set-piece organisation rather than attacking set-piece invention, because the attacking routines have already professionalised and the defending has not. Every serious club side now has a version of Jover's blocking structure. Almost nobody employs a coach whose only job is stopping it. Slovakia's flaw sat in public for four years and cost them a quarter-final. The same audit exists right now for at least a dozen national teams, and my guess is that it will still be sitting there in June 2028.

Set pieces did not win Euro 2024 because they are football's decisive weapon. They won it because they were the one phase of the game where a competent plan met no plan at all. That is an unclaimed edge, and it has been lying there since the 57th second of a Tuesday night in Leipzig.
