The consensus going into this World Cup was simple and, on the surface, well-earned: Julian Nagelsmann had finally solved Germany. Eight years after Die Mannschaft crashed out of the 2018 tournament as defending champions, beaten by Mexico, beaten by South Korea, gone in the group stage for the first time since 1938. The story was that the rebuild had worked. Florian Wirtz and Jamal Musiala had been "unlocked" as a pairing, the gegenpressing identity was restored, Joshua Kimmich anchored a midfield that finally had balance, and this was, in the words of half the preview pieces published in May, a genuine contender for the trophy.

It was wrong. Not wrong in hindsight, either. Wrong in a way the data was already screaming before a ball had been kicked in the group stage, and wrong in a way that has now been confirmed in the most literal sense possible: Germany are out. They lost to Paraguay on penalties in the round of 32 on June 29, the first time in the country's World Cup history it has ever lost a shootout at the tournament. That is not an unlucky footnote to an otherwise sound campaign. It is the third data point in a pattern that the "rebuild" narrative was built specifically to ignore.

## Did Nagelsmann actually fix what went wrong in 2018?

No. The evidence says the underlying fragility that produced the 2018 collapse was never addressed. It was just papered over by an easy qualifying draw, and it resurfaced the moment Germany faced any real pressure.

Start with September 4, 2025. Germany traveled to Bratislava for the opening match of World Cup qualifying and lost 2-0 to Slovakia, goals from David Hancko and David Strelec. This was not a rotated, dead-rubber Germany side experimenting with a back three. This was Nagelsmann's first competitive fixture of the cycle, and it produced Germany's first-ever away defeat in World Cup qualifying history, a run stretching back to 1934, and their first-ever loss to Slovakia. It came on the back of losses to Portugal and France in the Nations League that same summer, meaning Germany had lost three straight competitive matches for the first time in their 117-year existence as a football federation. Nagelsmann's own words afterward were not about shape or personnel: "We did not show any emotionality in our game today. In terms of emotions the opponents were miles ahead of us." That is a confession about temperament, not tactics, and temperament is precisely what went missing against South Korea in Kazan in 2018.

Germany recovered to win the group, five wins from six after that opener, capped by a 6-0 demolition of Slovakia on the final matchday with goals from Leroy Sané (2), Nick Woltemade, Serge Gnabry, Ridle Baku and Assan Ouedraogo. That scoreline is the one the "rebuild" case leans on. But a dead-rubber 6-0 against a side already through tells you far less than an away collapse against the same opponent when it mattered. The consensus took the second result as proof of correction and discarded the first as noise. It should have been the other way around.

## How did Germany's group stage at the tournament actually go?

It went the way qualifying did: comfortable against weak opposition, shaky against anyone competent, and it ended in an outright loss to a team they didn't need to beat. Germany opened with a 7-1 win over Curaçao (their first-ever World Cup appearance, a nation of 150,000 people), which tells you nothing about a knockout run. They then needed a 2-1 win to get past Côte d'Ivoire. In their final group match, already qualified as group winners, they lost 2-1 to Ecuador in New Jersey. Sané had given Germany an early lead; Nilson Angulo equalized within nine minutes, beating Aleksandar Pavlovic and finding the corner past Manuel Neuer; Gonzalo Plata scored the winner with 13 minutes left after Germany had a second-half penalty overturned by VAR. It was Germany's first group-stage loss since Japan beat them in Qatar in 2022, a fact that should have been treated as an alarm, not an asterisk, given the "already qualified" excuse was doing all the work in the post-match coverage.

Ecuador's win was also historically loaded: it was their first victory over UEFA opposition since 2013. A team that hadn't beaten a European side in over a decade beat a Germany side supposedly rebuilt for exactly this kind of test. If the rebuild were real, that result shouldn't be possible regardless of the dead-rubber context, a functioning knockout team manages a low-stakes fixture without conceding its shape entirely.

## What actually happened against Paraguay in the round of 32?

Germany dominated the ball, created little of real quality, had a legitimate-looking goal chalked off, and then lost the shootout, which is precisely the profile of a team whose attacking solution against a disciplined low block still does not exist.

Julio Enciso headed Paraguay in front against the run of play in the first half. Kai Havertz equalized early in the second. From there, Paraguay put in what was widely described as a defensive masterclass, ceding possession and territory but conceding almost nothing clean. In extra time, Jonathan Tah headed in from a corner. The goal was ruled out on VAR review for a marginal foul by Waldemar Anton on Paraguay's goalkeeper, Orlando Gill. It finished 1-1. Germany then lost the shootout 4-3, missing three of six attempts. Tah, whose header had just been controversially disallowed, blasted the decisive penalty over the bar in sudden death. José Canale scored the winner for Paraguay. It was, again, the first time in Germany's World Cup history they have lost a shootout at the tournament.

This is worth sitting with: this tournament as a whole produced the largest gap between goals scored and non-penalty expected goals ever measured at a World Cup, according to post-match data compiled by Total Football Analysis. Teams were finishing far above their underlying chance quality across the board. Against that backdrop, a Germany team that dominated the ball but couldn't turn territorial control into a clean win against a side "largely nullified" defensively wasn't unlucky. It was behind the tournament-wide curve on finishing precisely when it mattered most.

## Is the penalty shootout loss just bad luck?

No, treat it as an isolated coin flip and you miss that this is now the fourth consecutive major tournament in which Germany's attack has failed to solve a set defensive problem at the exact moment the stakes rose. Bad luck doesn't repeat with this consistency.

Group-stage elimination in 2018. Group-stage elimination in 2022. A quarterfinal exit to Spain at Euro 2024, decided in extra time after Germany couldn't convert a second-half territorial advantage into a winner. And now a round-of-32 shootout loss to Paraguay in 2026, having needed VAR to strip them of what would have been a winning goal and having still missed three penalties when the moment arrived. Four tournaments, four different specific mechanisms of exit, one consistent underlying failure: Germany get to the moment where composure and a clear attacking answer against a set defense are the only things that matter, and they don't have either. That is a structural problem, not variance. Variance doesn't show up in the same team's biggest moments four tournaments running.

## Can Wirtz and Musiala's partnership really unlock a low block?

Not yet, and Paraguay is the proof. The pairing that was supposed to be Germany's release valve against exactly this kind of opponent produced one own-created goal across 120 minutes against a defense that was set up to concede possession and nothing else.

Nagelsmann's system in this cycle is built around a 4-2-3-1 that shifts into a 4-3-3 in possession, Kimmich screening in front of the back four while Wirtz and Musiala rotate freely off Woltemade or Havertz up top. Wirtz, 23, joined Liverpool for a reported £100 million and had 5 goals and 3 assists in the Premier League this season after a slow adjustment period. Musiala, 22, came into the tournament off a serious injury layoff. Woltemade, 24, Newcastle's club-record signing from Stuttgart, scored 8 goals with 3 assists in his debut Premier League season but was also public about going fourteen matches without a goal mid-season before recovering form in February. None of that is damning on its own, plenty of good teams carry players who are still building consistency. What is damning is that the tactical promise attached to this trio, that individual quality in the final third would substitute for a repeatable structural plan against a packed defense. Is exactly the same promise that was made about Mesut Özil and Thomas Müller in 2018, and about Musiala and Havertz in 2022. It didn't hold in either of those tournaments. It didn't hold against Paraguay, either.

## So what actually happens to Germany next?

The correct read is not that Germany have a talent problem (they clearly don't, not with Wirtz, Musiala and a captain of Kimmich's caliber), but that they have an unresolved composure-and-connection problem that has now outlasted three different tournament cycles, two managers, and one supposedly complete rebuild. Nagelsmann's system is a genuine tactical upgrade over the drift of the late Löw years: the pressing is sharper, the qualifying goal difference was real, the front three can be devastating against space. But the diagnosis that mattered, why Germany keep failing at the precise moment a match tightens, was never actually treated. It was assumed the talent upgrade would fix it by itself.

It didn't. Expect the same script at Euro 2028: comfortable wins over weak opposition, a scare or a loss against a mid-table side they should beat, and an exit against a team playing beneath them on paper, decided by the one skill nobody has coached into this squad. What to do when the game refuses to open up. Until the federation treats that as the actual problem rather than a footnote to good underlying numbers, the trophy drought that started in 2014 keeps running, tactics or no tactics.
