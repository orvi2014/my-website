The story usually starts in the summer of 1987, when Arrigo Sacchi arrived from Parma. He buried catenaccio, taught eleven men to defend inside a 25-metre block and press the ball as a unit, and Milan became unbeatable. Coaching courses still teach it that way. So does every anniversary piece.

The trouble is the league table. Sacchi spent four seasons at Milan and won one Serie A title, the first one, in 1987-88. After that: third in 1988-89, second in 1989-90, second again in 1990-91, five points behind Sampdoria. The coach we remember for the most dominant club side in living memory finished outside the top two in Italy as often as he finished top of it.

That gap is worth pulling at. Separate what Sacchi actually did from what the dynasty actually was and you find something else doing the heavy lifting. That something, rather than the pressing, is why no team since 1994 has managed the same trick.

## Did Arrigo Sacchi actually dominate Italian football?

No. One Scudetto in four seasons. Fabio Capello, who replaced him in 1991, won four in five.

Sacchi's Milan were a European side that happened to spend its weekends in Italy. They took the 1988-89 European Cup after the most violent 90 minutes of the era, a 5-0 dismantling of Real Madrid at San Siro on 19 April 1989 in front of 73,112, Carlo Ancelotti opening from distance, Frank Rijkaard and Ruud Gullit heading in before the break, Marco van Basten and Roberto Donadoni finishing it off ([UEFA match record](https://www.uefa.com/uefachampionsleague/match/881--milan-vs-real-madrid/)). Then Steaua Bucharest, beaten 4-0 at the Camp Nou on 24 May 1989. Then Benfica, 1-0 at the Praterstadion in Vienna on 23 May 1990, Rijkaard running onto van Basten's lay-off in the 68th minute.

Three enormous European nights. Napoli and Sampdoria were winning the league.

The European run nearly didn't happen at all. In the second round in November 1988, Milan were losing in Belgrade to Red Star, down to ten men after Pietro Paolo Virdis was sent off, behind to a Dejan Savićević goal the bench could barely see, when the fog came down and the referee abandoned the tie on 65 minutes. Replayed the following afternoon, it finished 1-1. Milan went through 4-2 on penalties. The greatest side of the modern era survived its first knockout round because of weather.

None of which makes the tactical revolution fake. It just means the revolution, on its own, did not produce dominance.

## Who really invented Milan's pressing game?

Viktor Maslov did, at Dynamo Kyiv, about twenty years earlier. Maslov's teams pressed the ball, marked zonally, pushed the full-backs on, and won three straight Soviet titles in 1966, 1967 and 1968.

The overlap is close to exact. Maslov had already pulled the wingers back into a 4-4-2, already told defenders to hold a zone rather than a man, already asked the whole side to squeeze the space in front of the ball. That is essentially the full list of things Milan were praised for in 1989 ([Sports Illustrated on Maslov](https://www.si.com/soccer/2019/06/28/viktor-maslov-soviet-pioneer-4-4-2-formation-inventor-pressing)). Rinus Michels carried the same principles to Ajax and the Netherlands. Sacchi, by his own account, watched that Dutch football obsessively, then bought three of its heirs.

What happened to Maslov afterwards is the useful bit. Nobody built him a statue. The ideas got absorbed, pressing became the ambient condition of European football, and within a decade you couldn't win anything with it because everyone had it. The innovation stopped being an edge the moment it became the standard.

Sacchi's version went the same way, faster. By the mid-1990s the compact block and the high line were common property. Pressing today isn't an advantage, it's the entry fee. So any account of the Milan dynasty built on pressing has to explain how the dynasty outlived the advantage.

## Was Milan's best team the one that pressed, or the one that scored 36 goals?

The one that scored 36. Milan took the 1993-94 Scudetto with 36 goals in 34 matches, conceded 15, the fewest in Serie A history, and then won the European Cup 4-0.

This is the part that tends to get skipped. The peak of the whole thing, the season that ends with Athens, was the least Sacchian team of the six. Slower. More conservative. Perfectly happy to sit on 1-0. Capello took the block and stopped hunting with it. He used it to smother.

Before that came the run no Italian side has since approached: 58 Serie A matches unbeaten between 26 May 1991 and 21 March 1993, including a full 34-game season without defeat in 1991-92, ended eventually by Parma at San Siro ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/772344-longest-football-soccer-unbeaten-streak-by-a-serie-a-team)). Sacchi's Milan never had a stretch like that. Capello's had it twice over.

Then Athens, which is where the tactical account really strains, because Milan won it without the two players that account depends on. Franco Baresi and Alessandro Costacurta were both suspended for 18 May 1994. Capello put Filippo Galli alongside Paolo Maldini with Marcel Desailly screening in front, and Milan beat Johan Cruyff's Barcelona 4-0: Daniele Massaro twice before half-time, Savićević, whose goal had vanished into the Belgrade fog six years earlier, with a lob two minutes after it, Desailly running through for the fourth ([UEFA match record](https://www.uefa.com/uefachampionsleague/match/51078--milan-vs-barcelona/)).

Take out a sweeper and a first-choice centre-back and most tactical systems wobble. Milan won by four. Whatever was holding that team up wasn't a set of instructions. It was habit, laid down over years, and the difference matters for everything that comes next.

There's a counter-case, naturally. The 1993 final: Marseille 1-0 at the Olympiastadion in Munich on 26 May 1993, Basile Boli heading in on 43 minutes ([UEFA](https://www.uefa.com/uefachampionsleague/match/50054--marseille-vs-milan/)). Milan lost a European Cup final in the middle of the unbeaten run. Dynasties do lose finals. It's what makes 1989, 1990 and 1994 inside six seasons remarkable rather than inevitable.

## Wasn't this just Silvio Berlusconi's money?

Partly, and the limit on it is the whole point. Berlusconi's money bought Gullit, van Basten and Rijkaard. Then Serie A's three-foreigner cap made a fourth purchase illegal at any price.

This is the objection I hear most, usually from people who'd like Milan filed alongside the modern super-clubs, and it collapses as soon as you count. Under the quota that ran through the dynasty years, a Serie A squad could field three non-Italians. Milan spent theirs on the three best attacking players in Europe and had no lever left. The other eight starters had to come from inside Italy, and in practice from inside Milan.

So the cheque bought a Dutch front three, and the regulation bought something no cheque could. Mauro Tassotti, at the club since 1980. Baresi, there since 1977. Maldini, debut at 16 in January 1985. Costacurta, out of the youth system. That back four played together, in the same shape, under two managers, for the better part of a decade. Capello inherited it fully formed, which is exactly why he could change the team's temperament without touching its spine.

Money didn't build that. A rule that made money useless past a certain point built that.

## Why has no team since 1994 come close?

Because the constraint that produced Milan was deleted. The Bosman ruling in December 1995 scrapped nationality quotas for EU players and killed the transfer fee for players out of contract, and squad continuity at that depth became structurally impossible at the top of the game.

Look at what "dynasty" has meant since. Real Madrid won three consecutive Champions Leagues in 2016, 2017 and 2018, something Milan never did. But in 2017-18 they finished third in La Liga on 76 points, 17 behind Barcelona's 93 ([FBref](https://fbref.com/en/squads/53a2f082/2017-2018/Real-Madrid-Stats)). Pep Guardiola's Barcelona were arguably better football than anything Milan produced, and held together for four seasons before scattering. Both sides are magnificent. Neither belongs in the same category, because neither combined domestic suffocation with continental ownership over six years using more or less the same eleven people.

The unreplicated thing isn't the trophies and it isn't the tactics. It's the continuity. After Bosman, a defender who plays like Baresi gets bought within two seasons, and the selling club signs three replacements with the money. The market clears too quickly for a spine to set.

## So where does the next dynasty actually come from?

From wherever a coach is forbidden to buy. Which now means international football, and which is why Sacchi's method found its purest expression not at Milan but with Italy, beaten on penalties by Brazil in the 1994 World Cup final at the Rose Bowl.

Put plainly: AC Milan 1988-1994 was not a tactical revolution that produced a dynasty. It was a dynasty, an unpurchasable, quota-enforced run of continuity among Italian defenders, stable enough to host a tactical revolution without breaking. First Sacchi's aggressive version, then Capello's conservative one. The pressing was borrowed from Maslov and Michels. The stability was homegrown, and the stability is what the trophy cabinet is really recording.

Maslov's story tells you what comes next, too. Once the innovation goes universal, the advantage moves to whoever still holds the scarce input. In 2026 the scarce input is time with the same players.

Spain won the World Cup on 19 July 2026, beating Argentina 1-0 after extra time at MetLife Stadium, Ferran Torres scoring in the 106th minute ([FIFA](https://www.fifa.com/en/match-centre/match/17/285023/289292/400021543)). Luis de la Fuente could not sign a single player. What that staff could do was hold a group together through age groups and tournaments until the automatisms became reflex, which is the Milan condition arrived at through a different rule.

My prediction is narrow enough to be wrong, which is the point of making it. The next thing that genuinely resembles Milan 1988-94 won't be a super-club with a smarter pressing scheme. It'll be a national team, or a club that squad-cost regulation forces to stop churning and start keeping. Watch the sides that can't spend. That's where dynasties live now. It's where they lived in 1966, and in 1989, whether or not anyone wrote it up that way at the time.
